Outlining and Reverse Outlining

Plan ideas before drafting and diagnose structure after drafting.

Plan ideas before drafting and diagnose structure after drafting.

← All foundational skills

The essential idea: An outline is a map of the ideas you intend to write. A reverse outline is a map of what an existing draft actually does. Both show how claims, evidence, and sections relate, and comparing the two shows you where to revise.

Why outline at all?

Writing asks you to do several demanding things at once: generate ideas, organize them, turn them into sentences, and check what you’ve written. Trying to do all of that simultaneously can overload your attention. In experiments with college writers, preparing an outline before drafting led to higher-quality documents. A mental outline helped as much as a written one, which suggests the benefit comes from doing the planning, not from having a page to look at (Kellogg, 1988).

Reverse outlining addresses a different problem. Experienced writers use revision to rethink the argument and move or merge whole sections, while student writers tend to treat revision as rewording individual sentences (Sommers, 1980). Because a reverse outline strips a draft down to its paragraph-level moves, it lets you see and revise structure instead of only polishing sentences.

Core practices

1. Forward outline: plan before drafting

Start with your purpose or central question, then arrange the sections and the evidence each one needs.

  1. Write your working claim or question in one or two sentences at the top. For a research paper this is your working thesis. For a report or proposal it is the main question, problem, or recommendation.
  2. List the main points that a reader would need in order to accept or understand that claim.
  3. Order them for the reader. Common patterns include general → specific, problem → cause → solution, chronological, compare and contrast, and evidence → recommendation.
  4. Under each point, note the evidence you’ll use (sources, data, examples) and any gaps you still need to fill.
  5. Draft topic sentences for each section. These become the skeleton of your paragraphs.

Choosing an outline format

Format What it looks like Good for
Topic outline Short phrases in a hierarchy (I, A, 1, a) Fast planning, early stages
Sentence outline A full sentence for each point Testing whether your argument holds together
Evidence outline Each point paired with its sources or data Research papers and literature reviews
Visual outline A mind map, flowchart, or sticky notes Exploring connections before committing to an order

The levels match outlining as a note-taking method (see Note Taking Methods): Level 1 for main sections, Level 2 for sub-points, Level 3 for supporting details, and Level 4 for examples.

2. Reverse outline: diagnose after drafting

A reverse outline is created from a draft. For each paragraph, write down what it says and what job it does in the argument.

  1. Number every paragraph in the draft.
  2. For each paragraph, write two short notes in the margin or in a two-column table:
    • Says: the main point, in one sentence.
    • Does: its function (introduces, defines, gives evidence, compares, counters, transitions, concludes).
  3. Read the list of notes on its own, without the draft. Does it make sense as an argument?
  4. Compare it with your thesis or purpose. Does every paragraph serve it?

1 Coastal erosion threatens county infrastructure Introduces problem Yes Thesis comes late in ¶. Move it up?

3. Revise the architecture

Use the reverse outline to look for these problems:

  • Repetition: Two or more paragraphs make the same point. Combine them.
  • Gaps: A step in the reasoning is missing between two paragraphs. Add a paragraph or a stronger transition.
  • Paragraphs doing too many jobs: The “Does” column needs “and.” Split the paragraph.
  • Off-thesis paragraphs: A paragraph doesn’t connect to your claim. Cut it, or revise the thesis if the paragraph shows where your thinking actually went.
  • Order problems: A paragraph relies on information that comes later. Move it.
  • Imbalance: A minor point gets three paragraphs and a major point gets one. Rebalance.

Make structural changes before editing sentences. There’s no point polishing a paragraph you’re about to cut.

A simple workflow

Before drafting (forward outline):

  1. Write your working claim or question.
  2. List three to six main points, in the order a reader needs them.
  3. Attach evidence to each point and flag gaps.
  4. Draft topic sentences. Treat the outline as a plan, not a contract.

After drafting (reverse outline):

  1. Number the paragraphs.
  2. Write each paragraph’s point and function in one line.
  3. Read the outline alone. Look for repetition, gaps, unsupported transitions, off-thesis paragraphs, and paragraphs doing too many jobs.
  4. Write a short revision plan: move, combine, split, add, or cut.
  5. Revise the structure, then reverse-outline again if the changes were large.

Using reverse outlines beyond your own drafts

  • Reading: Reverse-outline a dense article to see how the author builds an argument. You can borrow the structure for your own writing.
  • Peer review: Reverse-outline a classmate’s draft and share it with them. Showing a writer what each paragraph seems to do is often more useful than general comments (see Peer Review and Revision).
  • Comparing plan and draft: Put your original outline beside the reverse outline. Where they differ, decide which version is better. Sometimes the draft found a stronger argument than the plan.

In context

A draft about coastal erosion describes land-use history in three separated paragraphs. A reverse outline makes the repetition visible and suggests consolidating the history before comparing management responses. The writer also notices that one paragraph both compares options and recommends one. They split it so that the recommendation gets its own paragraph near the end, after the evidence.

A student writing a history essay on a labor strike drafts in chronological order. Their reverse outline shows that the argument about why the strike succeeded appears only in the last two paragraphs. They reorganize around three causes, using chronology within each section, so that the argument leads the essay.

Common pitfalls

  • Over-planning: Spending so long on the outline that drafting never starts. A working outline only needs to be good enough to begin.
  • Treating the outline as fixed: Drafting often shows you better ideas. Update the outline instead of forcing the draft to match it.
  • Summarizing content instead of function: In a reverse outline, “talks about sediment” is less useful than “gives evidence that sediment loss accelerated after 1990.”
  • Skipping the reverse outline because the draft “feels fine”: Structural problems are hard to see from inside the prose.

Keep in mind

An outline is a flexible hypothesis about organization, not a rule that prevents new thinking. Many writers discover their argument while drafting. The reverse outline is how you make sure the final structure reflects what you discovered.
