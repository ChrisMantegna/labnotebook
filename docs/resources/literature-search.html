Reproducible Literature Searching

Make the path from question to source selection understandable and repeatable.

Make the path from question to source selection understandable and repeatable.

← All foundational skills

The essential idea: A transparent literature search records where, when, and how you searched, and why sources were included or excluded. It lets you (or a reader, instructor, or teammate) see how you got from a question to a set of sources. It supports defensible decisions even when the search evolves.

Why make a search reproducible?

Most student searches are a string of quick queries that can’t be rebuilt a week later. That causes practical problems. You lose track of what you already checked, you can’t explain why a key paper is missing, and you can’t update the search when an assignment grows. Even in published systematic reviews, the search is often poorly reported, which led to the PRISMA-S reporting checklist (Rethlefsen et al., 2021).

You don’t need full systematic-review rigor for a course paper. The same habits scale down well: plan the question, build the search deliberately, and write down what you did.

Core practices

1. Scope the question

Before you search, write your question as one sentence. Then break it into concepts. A simple frame:

Element Ask yourself Example (eelgrass restoration)
Topic or intervention What phenomenon, process, or action? Eelgrass restoration / transplanting
Population or system Who or what is studied? Zostera marina beds
Context Where, when, under what conditions? Temperate estuaries
Outcome or focus What are you trying to learn about it? Sediment characteristics, survival
Evidence type What kind of source answers this? Field studies, experiments, reviews

Health sciences often use PICO (Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome) for the same purpose. Use whatever frame your discipline uses. What matters is having named concepts to search for.

Also decide your inclusion and exclusion criteria before you start screening: date range, language, study type, geography, peer review. Writing them down early keeps you from changing the rules to fit the sources you happen to find.

2. Build the search string

Combine synonyms within a concept using OR, and different concepts using AND. Do this in a text document first, not in the search box. A document lets you edit, reuse, and record the string (Bramer et al., 2018).

(eelgrass OR "Zostera marina" OR seagrass)
AND (restor* OR transplant* OR replant*)
AND (sediment* OR "grain size" OR "organic matter")

Tools to know (check each database’s help page, because syntax varies):

  • Quotation marks search an exact phrase: "grain size".
  • Truncation (often *) finds word endings: restor* → restore, restoration, restoring.
  • Parentheses group OR terms so AND applies correctly.
  • NOT excludes terms. Use it sparingly, since it can quietly remove relevant results.
  • Field limits restrict terms to title, abstract, or keywords.
  • Controlled vocabulary (subject headings such as MeSH in PubMed, or a database’s thesaurus) catches papers that use different words for the same idea.

Finding synonyms: Read the titles, abstracts, and keywords of two or three relevant papers. Note the terms they use, including older terms, regional spellings, and technical vs. common names.

3. Choose where to search

Use more than one source. Different databases index different journals, and no single tool covers everything.

  • A subject database for your field (e.g., Web of Science, Scopus, PubMed, ERIC, PsycINFO, Aquatic Sciences and Fisheries Abstracts). Your library’s subject guides list the main ones.
  • A broad tool such as Google Scholar, for breadth and gray literature. Its results change over time and are hard to reproduce, so log the date and treat it as a supplement.
  • Citation chasing: Check the reference lists of key papers (backward) and use “cited by” links to find newer work that cites them (forward).

Ask a librarian. Subject librarians help with exactly this, and a short consultation can save you hours.

4. Log your decisions

Keep a search log in a spreadsheet or document as you go. Record every search you run, including the ones that didn’t work.

Date Database / platform Exact search string Fields searched Filters / limits Results Notes / changes and why
2026-02-03 Web of Science Core Collection (eelgrass OR "Zostera marina") AND restor* AND sediment* Topic 2000–2026; English 214 Many results on terrestrial grasses. Added seagrass and field-limited to abstract.
2026-02-03 Web of Science Core Collection (eelgrass OR "Zostera marina" OR seagrass) AND (restor* OR transplant*) AND sediment* Abstract 2000–2026; English 167 More focused. Screening a sample of 30.

These fields line up with the core items PRISMA-S asks review authors to report: information sources, full search strategies, limits, and dates (Rethlefsen et al., 2021).

5. Screen in stages, and record your reasons

  1. Title and abstract screening: Apply your inclusion and exclusion criteria. Mark each source include, exclude, or maybe.
  2. Full-text screening: Read the “maybes” and includes. For each exclusion, record a short reason (“terrestrial system,” “no sediment data,” “opinion piece”).
  3. Count as you go. Note how many records you found, removed as duplicates, screened, excluded (with reasons), and kept. These counts are the same flow PRISMA diagrams report (Page et al., 2021), and they let you describe your search in one sentence of a methods section or paper.

6. Manage sources with a reference manager

Export results into a reference manager such as Zotero (free), Mendeley, or EndNote. A reference manager removes duplicates, stores PDFs and notes, and formats citations. Use folders or tags that mirror your screening stages (e.g., to-screen, included, excluded-scope).

A simple workflow

  1. Write the question and break it into three or four concepts.
  2. List synonyms for each concept, and set inclusion criteria.
  3. Draft the search string in a document.
  4. Run the search in one database. Log the exact string, settings, date, and result count.
  5. Scan the first 20–30 results. Are they on target? What terms do relevant papers use that you missed?
  6. Revise one element at a time (add a synonym, change a field limit, adjust a filter). Rerun it, log it, and note why you changed it. Changing several things at once makes it impossible to tell what helped.
  7. Translate the final string to a second database, adjusting the syntax, and log it.
  8. Screen, record reasons, and chase citations from your best sources.

Knowing when to stop

You will rarely find “everything.” Signs that a search is good enough for its purpose:

  • New searches and citation chasing mostly return sources you’ve already seen.
  • The key papers your instructor, a review article, or a librarian would expect are in your set.
  • You can explain the boundaries of your search in two or three sentences.

In context

For a question about eelgrass restoration and sediment, a student searches two databases and logs both query syntaxes, because one platform uses field codes differently from the other. Their first search returns many studies of terrestrial grasses. They record the problem, add seagrass and "Zostera marina", limit terms to abstracts, and note the change in the log. During screening they record “terrestrial system” as an exclusion reason for six papers, so their final set and their choices are easy to explain.

A student in a public policy course is researching minimum-wage effects on youth employment. They search a social science database and a working-paper repository, and they log that they limited results to 2010 onward because of a major policy change that year. When a classmate asks why an older landmark study is missing, the log explains the decision.

Common pitfalls

  • Searching a full sentence. Databases match keywords, not questions. Break the question into concepts.
  • Stopping at the first page of results. Relevance ranking isn’t the same as relevance to your question.
  • Relying on one database. Coverage differs. A second source often turns up key papers.
  • Not saving the string. If you can’t rerun it, you can’t update or explain it.
  • Changing criteria mid-screening to keep a favorite paper or drop an inconvenient one.

Keep in mind

A documented search is not automatically a systematic review. Systematic, scoping, and other formal reviews have their own design, reporting, and quality-control standards, such as protocols, independent dual screening, and critical appraisal (Grant & Booth, 2009; Page et al., 2021). Describe your work accurately. “A structured search of two databases” is honest and defensible. Calling it “a systematic review” when it isn’t one is not.
