Ethical Research, Writing, and AI Use

Build trustworthy practices for attribution, representation, and responsible tool use.

Build trustworthy practices for attribution, representation, and responsible tool use.

← All foundational skills

The essential idea: Ethical communication requires accurate attribution, honest representation of evidence, care for collaborators and communities, and transparency about the tools used to produce the work.

Why this matters

Research and writing depend on trust. Readers trust that your sources say what you claim, that your data are reported honestly, that the people and places you describe were treated with care, and that your work is your own unless you say otherwise. Most ethical problems in student work aren’t deliberate. They come from rushed note taking, unclear expectations, or tools used without checking. The practices on this page are habits that prevent those problems.

Core practices

1. Attribute appropriately

Cite sources for ideas, data, images, figures, and language, not just for direct quotations.

What you’re using What attribution requires
Exact words Quotation marks + citation + page number
A paraphrase or summary Citation (and page number if your style requires)
Someone’s idea, argument, or framework Citation, even if you’ve reworded it completely
Data, statistics, figures, maps, images Citation; permission or license for reuse where required
Your own previous work Check course or journal policy before reusing it
Common knowledge in your field Usually no citation, but when in doubt, cite
  • Paraphrase properly: Change both the words and the sentence structure, and still cite. Swapping a few words in someone else’s sentence is still too close to the original.
  • Distinguish your analysis from others’ work. Use signal phrases (“Lee argues…”, “In contrast, I suggest…”) so readers can tell whose idea is whose.
  • Check that citations support claims. Citation errors are common even in published research (Mogull, 2017). Read the cited passage before you rely on it.

2. Represent evidence honestly

  • Don’t fabricate, falsify, or selectively omit data or results.
  • Report uncertainty and limitations, including findings that don’t fit your argument.
  • Keep figures and images honest: consistent scales, labeled axes, no misleading crops, and no manipulation that changes what the data show.
  • Avoid overstating: Match your language to the strength of the evidence (“suggests” vs. “proves”).
  • Credit contributors accurately in group work and co-authored projects, and agree on roles and authorship early.

3. Respect people, communities, and materials

Research with people. If your project involves surveys, interviews, observations, or data about people, it may need review by your institution’s Institutional Review Board (IRB) or ethics office, even for a class project. Ask your instructor before you begin. The Belmont Report, the foundation of US human-subjects regulation, rests on three principles (National Commission, 1979):

  • Respect for persons: informed, voluntary consent, and protection for people with diminished autonomy.
  • Beneficence: do no harm, and maximize benefits while minimizing risks.
  • Justice: fair distribution of research burdens and benefits.

Privacy and confidentiality. Protect personal, sensitive, and confidential information. Remove identifiers, store data securely, and share only what consent and data-use agreements permit.

Community-controlled and Indigenous data. Some data and knowledge belong to communities, not to individual researchers. The CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance (Collective Benefit, Authority to Control, Responsibility, Ethics) complement the FAIR principles for open data, and ask researchers to be both “FAIR and CARE” (Carroll et al., 2020). In practice, follow community protocols, data agreements, and consent conditions, and ask before sharing.

Sensitive locations and species. Publishing precise locations of rare or endangered species has been linked to poaching and habitat disturbance. Some researchers recommend buffering spatial data or providing only broad coordinates (Lindenmayer & Scheele, 2017). Apply the same care to archaeological, cultural, and sacred sites.

4. Use AI tools critically and transparently

Generative AI tools can help you brainstorm, summarize, or edit, but they also create specific risks.

They can invent sources. In one study, 55% of the citations generated by GPT-3.5 and 18% of those generated by GPT-4 were fabricated, and many real citations contained substantive errors (Walters & Wilder, 2023). Never cite a source you haven’t found and read yourself.

They can’t take responsibility for the work. Publication-ethics bodies such as COPE state that AI tools can’t be listed as authors, because they can’t take responsibility for the work. Authors who use AI must disclose how, and they remain fully responsible for the content (COPE, 2023).

Practical guidelines:

  • Follow the policy for the specific setting. AI rules differ between courses, assignments, journals, and employers. If a policy isn’t stated, ask.
  • Verify every factual claim and citation independently, using primary sources.
  • Don’t share restricted content with AI tools: unpublished data, participant information, confidential documents, peer-review materials, or a classmate’s work without permission.
  • Disclose use when required, and cite AI output in the way your style guide specifies. APA, for example, provides guidance for citing generative AI (McAdoo, 2023).
  • Keep a short record of what tool you used, for what task, and what you checked. A record makes disclosure easy and accurate.

Example AI-use record:

Tool and date: [AI tool], 3 Feb. Task: Asked for alternative organizational structures for my literature review. What I used: I adopted the idea of grouping by method rather than by date. What I checked: No sources or facts came from the tool. All citations are from my own search.

5. Respect copyright and licensing

  • Check reuse rights for images, figures, and media. Look for Creative Commons licenses and follow their terms (attribution, non-commercial, share-alike).
  • Educational use is not a blanket exemption, especially for work posted publicly.
  • When in doubt, link to the original rather than reproducing it, or create your own figure and cite the data source.

A simple workflow: the ethics audit

Before submitting or sharing a draft, check:

  1. Claims: For each factual claim, can you find its source? Does the source say what you claim?
  2. Quotations and paraphrases: Are quotes marked and cited with page numbers? Are paraphrases in your own words and cited?
  3. Visuals: Is each figure, image, or map cited, permitted, and honestly presented?
  4. Data and people: Are personal, sensitive, or location data protected? Do you have the required approvals?
  5. Tools: Is any AI assistance used within policy, disclosed where required, and independently checked?
  6. Contributions: Are collaborators and data sources credited?

In context

A student preparing a public map of ecological observations asks whether showing precise coordinates could expose sensitive species or culturally significant sites. They choose to aggregate points to a broader geographic scale and note this choice in the map’s caption.

A student uses an AI tool to suggest search terms for a literature review. It also offers three “key studies.” When they check, one doesn’t exist and another reports a different finding than described. The student cites only sources they found and read, and notes their limited AI use in a short disclosure as the course requires.

For a public health class, a student wants to interview classmates about sleep habits. Before recruiting, they ask their instructor whether the project needs ethics review, prepare a consent statement, and plan to report results without names.

Common pitfalls

  • “Patchwriting”: stitching together lightly reworded phrases from sources. This often happens when notes don’t separate quotations from paraphrase.
  • Citing what you haven’t read, including citations copied from another paper’s reference list or from an AI tool.
  • Assuming class projects are exempt from human-subjects or privacy rules.
  • Posting images found online without checking the license.
  • Undisclosed AI use where disclosure is required, or assuming one course’s policy applies to another.

Keep in mind

Course, journal, institutional, funder, and community protocols may differ. Follow the requirements of the specific project, and seek guidance from your instructor, librarian, ethics office, or community partners when obligations conflict.
