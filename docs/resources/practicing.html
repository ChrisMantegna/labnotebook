Practicing the Skills

Independent exercises and reusable working templates

Self-directed practice across the foundational skills library.

← All foundational skills

How to use this module: Each activity takes about 15–30 minutes, with optional extensions. Choose the activity that fits your current work. You don’t need to do them in order. Use real material from your own courses or projects whenever you can, and keep your outputs in a notebook or project folder so your decisions stay traceable.

Templates: Practice log · Literature search log · Synthesis matrix · Revision plan · AI use log

Compare note-taking approaches

Connected guide: Note Taking Methods · Time: 25–30 minutes · You’ll need: one short reading (3–6 pages) from any course

Task: Practice choosing a method by purpose, and see what different methods help you notice.

  1. Write your purpose at the top of a scratch page in one sentence.
  2. Skim once and fill only two columns: conceptual questions and practical questions. Take no other notes.
  3. Read one section using Cornell notes. Record in the right column, then reduce to cues in the left column.
  4. Read a second section using annotation. Mark claims (C), evidence (E), assumptions (A), and limitations (L), with a margin note for every mark.
  5. Recite: Cover your Cornell notes and answer your cue questions from memory. Note what you couldn’t recall.
  6. Compare: What did each method help you notice? Which fit your purpose better?

Suggested record:

Section Purpose Method What it helped me notice What it missed Recall check (✓ / ✗)
Cornell
Annotation

Check your work:

  • Quotations, paraphrases, and your own ideas are visibly separate, with page numbers.
  • Your Cornell summary is in your own words and is three sentences or fewer.
  • Every annotation mark has a note explaining why you made it.

Extension: Turn the same reading into a mind map with labeled links, then review your Cornell cues again two or three days later before rereading anything.

Reflect: Which practical questions did the reading answer, and which conceptual questions are still open? Which method will you use next time for this kind of reading, and why?

Run three documented search iterations

Connected guide: Reproducible Literature Searching · Time: 30 minutes · You’ll need: access to a library database, plus the literature search log

Task: Build a search deliberately and record each change so that someone else could rerun it.

  1. Write a focused question in one sentence and split it into three concepts.
  2. List synonyms for each concept, using two or three relevant abstracts to find terms you hadn’t thought of.
  3. Write inclusion and exclusion criteria (date range, study type, setting) before searching.
  4. Search 1: Draft the string in a document using OR within concepts and AND between them. Run it, then log the exact string, database, date, fields, filters, and result count.
  5. Scan the first 20 results. Note what’s on target, what’s off, and which terms relevant papers use.
  6. Searches 2 and 3: Change one element at a time (add a synonym, change a field limit, or adjust a filter). Log each change and why you made it.
  7. Screen 10 titles and abstracts from your best search: include, exclude (with a reason), or maybe.

Suggested record:

Date Database Exact query Fields Filters Results Change and reason

Check your work:

  • Could a classmate rerun every search from your log alone?
  • Did each iteration change only one thing?
  • Does every exclusion have a recorded reason?

Extension: Translate your best string into a second database, adjusting the syntax. Then chase citations from your strongest source, both backward through its reference list and forward through “cited by.”

Reflect: Which change had the biggest effect, and why? How would you describe the boundaries of your search in two sentences?

Audit five citations

Connected guide: Reference Managers · Time: 25 minutes · You’ll need: Zotero (or another reference manager) with its browser connector and word-processor plugin installed

Task: Import sources, verify their records, and check that a generated bibliography is accurate.

  1. Create a collection for a current project.
  2. Import five sources using at least two methods: a database page, a DOI, and an ISBN or library catalog entry.
  3. Verify each record against the source itself: authors, title, publication, volume and pages, date, item type, and DOI.
  4. Correct errors in the library (not in the document), and add one or two tags from a short, consistent list.
  5. Add a note to each item explaining why it matters to your project.
  6. Insert all five citations into a document with the plugin, then generate a bibliography in your required style.
  7. Spot-check two entries against the official style guide.

Suggested record:

Source identifier Import method Metadata errors found Correction made Tags Why it matters

Check your work:

  • Every item has a DOI or stable identifier where one exists. Zotero’s retraction check depends on it.
  • No item is the wrong type (for example, a book chapter saved as a journal article).
  • You didn’t edit the bibliography by hand.

Extension: Pick one sentence in a draft that cites a source. Reopen the source and confirm that the cited passage actually supports that specific claim.

Reflect: How many records needed corrections? What does that suggest about trusting automatic metadata?

Move from a matrix to a paragraph

Connected guide: Synthesizing Literature · Time: 30 minutes (plus reading time) · You’ll need: three sources on one shared question, plus the synthesis matrix

Task: Compare sources systematically and write one synthesis paragraph that starts from a claim, not from an author.

  1. Complete one matrix row per source using the same columns: question, methods, setting, findings, limitations, relevance.
  2. Read down the columns. Find one agreement and one apparent disagreement.
  3. Investigate the disagreement: Can design, setting, population, scale, or measurement explain it?
  4. Write a one-sentence cross-source claim.
  5. Write the paragraph: claim → evidence from at least two sources → complication → so what.
  6. Underline your claim and circle each author name. If a circled name starts the paragraph, revise.

Suggested record:

Source Question Method Setting Finding Limitation Connection to others

Check your work:

  • The paragraph opens with your observation, not a summary of one study.
  • At least two sources appear as evidence.
  • Your language matches the strength of the evidence (suggests vs. shows).
  • You don’t present three studies as the consensus of a whole field.

Extension: Add two more sources and a concept column for a theme that keeps coming up. Do your groupings change?

Reflect: What did reading down the columns show you that reading each source alone did not?

A seven-session writing experiment

Connected guide: Developing a Writing Habit · Time: 10–25 minutes per session, over 1–2 weeks · You’ll need: a current writing project of any size, plus the practice log

Task: Treat your writing routine as an experiment, and gather evidence about what helps you start and keep going.

  1. Set an if-then plan for each session: “If it’s [time/place], then I’ll write for [unit].”
  2. Choose a small unit: 10–25 minutes, one paragraph, or one question answered.
  3. Before each session, name one purpose: explore, explain, revise, or edit.
  4. During the session, stay with that purpose. Note anything else for later.
  5. End each session with a restart note: the next sentence, question, or source.
  6. Log each session. Vary one condition across sessions (place, time of day, music or silence, alone or with a group) so you can compare.
  7. After seven sessions, review the log for patterns.

Suggested record:

Date If-then plan Purpose Condition tested Time or output What helped Restart note

Check your work:

  • Every session ends with a restart note.
  • Missed sessions are logged with the reason, not just skipped.
  • At least two conditions were compared.

Extension: Join or start a writing session with others for at least two of the seven sessions, and compare those entries with your solo sessions.

Reflect: Which cue or condition most reliably got you started? What does a missed session tell you about your schedule, rather than about your ability?

Reverse-outline a draft

Connected guide: Outlining and Reverse Outlining · Time: 20–30 minutes · You’ll need: 5–8 consecutive paragraphs from your own draft or an openly available article

Task: Map what each paragraph actually does, then propose a better structure.

  1. Write the draft’s thesis or purpose in one sentence at the top.
  2. Number each paragraph.
  3. For each paragraph, write two notes: Says (main point, one sentence) and Does (function: introduces, gives evidence, compares, counters, concludes).
  4. Read only the outline, without the draft. Does it work as an argument?
  5. Mark problems: repetition, gaps, off-thesis paragraphs, paragraphs doing two jobs (“and” in the Does column), and order problems.
  6. Propose a revised order and a short action for each paragraph: keep, move, combine, split, add, or cut.

Suggested record:

¶ Says Does Evidence used Supports thesis? Action

Check your work:

  • Says entries describe claims (“evidence that X increased after 1990”), not just topics (“talks about X”).
  • Every paragraph has exactly one job, or is marked to split.
  • Structural actions are planned before any sentence editing.

Extension: Compare your reverse outline with your original plan. Where they differ, decide which version is stronger and why.

Reflect: What problem was invisible in the prose but obvious in the outline?

Feedback to revision plan

Connected guide: Peer Review and Revision · Time: 20 minutes to review, 20 minutes to plan · You’ll need: a short draft (1–3 pages), a peer, and the revision plan

Task: Exchange focused feedback and turn it into a prioritized, reasoned revision plan.

As the writer:

  1. Write a short note for your reviewer: your goal, your audience, and one place where you’re unsure.

As the reviewer:

  1. Read once without marking.
  2. Summarize the main message in two or three sentences.
  3. On a second pass, give one strength, two substantive comments, and one unanswered question. Tie each to a location in the draft and use the observe → effect → suggest pattern.

As the writer, again:

  1. Read all feedback before responding. Sort comments into global vs. local and agree / unsure / disagree.
  2. Compare the reviewer’s summary with your intended message.
  3. Write a revision plan with priorities and a reason for each decision.

Suggested record:

Priority Feedback Location Decision (accept / adapt / decline) Planned action Reason

Check your work:

  • Every comment points to a specific place in the draft.
  • Global issues are ranked above sentence-level issues.
  • Declined suggestions have a reason.

Extension: Swap roles and review your partner’s draft. Then write two sentences on what reviewing taught you about your own draft.

Reflect: Did the reviewer’s summary match your intended message? If not, what does that gap tell you?

Translate for two audiences

Connected guide: Communicating with Non-Expert Audiences · Time: 30 minutes · You’ll need: one clearly supported finding from a source you have read

Task: Adapt one finding for two different audiences while keeping its meaning and limits.

  1. Write the original finding in one sentence, including its conditions and uncertainty (where, when, how sure).
  2. Choose two specific audiences, for example a community meeting and an interdisciplinary research team, or a city council and a high school class.
  3. For each audience, fill in a Message Box: Problem, So what?, Solutions, Benefits.
  4. Draft a short version for each (a 100-word paragraph, a slide, or a 60-second spoken explanation), bottom line first.
  5. Check terms: Replace or define jargon, and watch for words with different public meanings (e.g., “significant,” “theory,” “positive feedback”).
  6. Fidelity check: Compare each version with your original sentence. Are the conditions and uncertainty still there?
  7. Test one version with someone from (or close to) that audience.

Suggested record:

Audience Goal So what? (for them) Key message Terms changed Uncertainty kept? Format

Check your work:

  • Each version leads with meaning, not methods.
  • The “so what?” differs between the two audiences.
  • Neither version claims more than the evidence supports.

Extension: Run both drafts through a jargon checker such as the De-Jargonizer and revise any flagged terms.

Reflect: What did you have to give up for each audience, and what did you refuse to give up?

Trace a claim and audit tool use

Connected guide: Ethical Research, Writing, and AI Use · Time: 25 minutes · You’ll need: one paragraph and one visual (figure, image, map, or table) from a current draft

Task: Verify where your content came from, whether it is represented fairly, and whether any tool use is documented.

  1. List every factual claim in the paragraph.
  2. Trace each claim to its source. Open the source and confirm it says what you claim, at the page you cite.
  3. Check wording: Mark direct quotes and check paraphrases against the original for patchwriting.
  4. Check the visual: Who created it? What license or permission applies? Is it cited? Does it present the data honestly (scales, axes, cropping)?
  5. Check data and people: Does anything identify individuals, sensitive locations, or community-controlled information?
  6. Record any tool assistance (AI, translation, paraphrasing, grammar tools), what it did, and what you verified.

Suggested record:

Item Source / permission Verified against original? Risk Action

Check your work:

  • Every claim traces to a source you have read.
  • The visual has a citation and a reuse right you can name.
  • Any tool use is recorded and allowed by the policy for this assignment.

Extension: Complete the Ethical AI use: vetting checklist below for a full assignment.

Reflect: Which item was hardest to trace? What note-taking or citation habit would have made it easier?

Connected guide: Ethical Research, Writing, and AI Use · Use: before you start using an AI tool on an assignment, and again before you submit · Template: AI use log

Example disclosure statement:

I used [tool name] on [date(s)] to [task, e.g., suggest alternative search terms and check grammar in my final draft]. I did not use it to generate sources, analysis, or text for [sections]. I verified all suggestions against my own reading, and all citations come from my own search.

If you can’t check a box: Stop and fix the issue, remove the AI-assisted material, or ask your instructor before submitting. An unchecked box is information, not a failure.

Practice reflection

After any activity, record three things: what you tried, what you noticed, and what you will do next. Every few weeks, look back across your practice log:

  • Which skills are becoming routine?
  • Which steps do you still skip under time pressure?
  • Which one habit would most improve your next project?

The goal is a usable process, not a perfect worksheet.
