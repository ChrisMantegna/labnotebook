Synthesizing Literature

Turn a collection of sources into a connected account of what is known.

Turn a collection of sources into a connected account of what is known.

← All foundational skills {.resource-back}

The essential idea: Synthesis compares evidence across sources to explain patterns, disagreements, limitations, and open questions. It is more than a sequence of article summaries. It is organized around ideas, not around authors.

Summary vs. synthesis

The most common problem in student literature reviews is writing an “annotated bibliography in paragraphs”: one paragraph per source, in the order the sources were read. Webster and Watson (2002) describe the difference as author-centric vs. concept-centric reviews. Strong reviews are organized by concept.

Summary (author-centric) Synthesis (concept-centric)
“Smith (2019) found… Lee (2021) found… Patel (2022) found…” “Three field studies report cooling from tree cover, but the size of the effect depends on how temperature was measured.”
One source per paragraph Several sources per paragraph, connected by a claim
Describes what each source says Explains how sources relate: agree, conflict, extend, or leave gaps
Ends when the sources run out Ends with what is still unknown and why it matters

Core practices

1. Start from your notes, organized for comparison

Synthesis depends on note taking. If your notes on each source record different things, you can’t compare them. Before building a matrix, make sure each source has notes on the same core questions. See Note Taking Methods for annotation and the conceptual and practical question columns that feed this step.

2. Build a comparison matrix

A synthesis matrix is a table with one row per source and the same columns for every source. The idea goes back to Garrard’s “matrix method” for literature reviews (Garrard, 2022) and to Webster and Watson’s concept matrix (2002). Matrix-style notes have also been linked to better recall and synthesis than conventional linear notes (Kiewra et al., 1991).

Start with columns like these, and adapt them to your field:

Source Question / aim Methods & design Setting / population / scale Key findings Limitations (stated & your own) Relevance to my question My notes / questions
Author A (2019)
Author B (2021)
Author C (2023)

Tips:

  • Use consistent wording within each column (e.g., always “field experiment,” “survey,” “model,” “lab study”). Consistent wording makes patterns visible.
  • Add concept columns for the themes that keep coming up, and mark which sources address each one. This is Webster and Watson’s concept matrix.
  • Separate what the source says from what you think. Keep your own interpretations in the last column.
  • Build the matrix in a spreadsheet so you can sort and filter by method, year, or setting.

3. Look across rows, not just along them

Reading along a row tells you about one study. Reading down a column shows patterns across studies. Ask:

  • Agreement: Which findings show up across several studies?
  • Divergence: Where do results differ? Do the differences line up with method, setting, population, scale, time period, or how the outcome was measured?
  • Gaps: Which questions, places, populations, or methods are missing?
  • Quality and weight: Which findings rest on stronger designs or larger samples? Which rest on a single study?
  • Change over time: Has the consensus shifted? Did a new method change the picture?

Group sources by what they share: a claim, a method, a scale, or a context. Those groups often become the sections of your review.

4. Write from a claim

Each synthesis paragraph should start with your cross-study observation, then use individual studies as evidence.

A paragraph pattern:

  1. Claim: A cross-source observation in your own words.
  2. Evidence: Two or more sources that support it, with brief specifics.
  3. Complication: Where sources differ, and the likely reason (design, site, measurement).
  4. So what: What this means for your question, or what remains unresolved.

Before (summary):

Author A (2019) measured air temperature under trees in a temperate city and found cooling of about 1 °C. Author B (2021) studied an arid city and found larger cooling. Author C (2023) used satellite data and found a strong effect.

After (synthesis):

Studies agree that tree cover lowers urban temperatures, but reported magnitudes differ in ways that track how temperature was measured. Ground-level air-temperature studies report modest cooling (Author A, 2019), whereas satellite-derived surface temperatures show larger differences (Author C, 2023), likely because surfaces heat and cool more than air. Climate may also matter: cooling appears greater in an arid city (Author B, 2021), though a single study can’t establish that pattern. Comparing these results requires attention to the outcome measured, not only the direction of the effect.

(Author names and values are placeholders that show the structure.)

5. Make your synthesis visible in the language

Use words that signal relationships between sources:

  • Agreement: consistent with, corroborates, similarly, converging evidence
  • Contrast: in contrast, however, diverges, whereas
  • Qualification: only under, depends on, limited to, in the settings studied
  • Extension: builds on, extends, applies … to a new context
  • Gap: few studies, remains unclear, has not been tested in

A simple workflow

  1. Choose three related sources.
  2. Complete a matrix with comparable fields for each.
  3. Read down the columns. Identify one agreement and one apparent disagreement.
  4. Investigate the disagreement. Can design, site, population, scale, or measurement explain it?
  5. Write one paragraph using the claim → evidence → complication → so what pattern.
  6. Add sources and repeat. As patterns emerge, group them into themes for your outline.

In context

Three urban heat studies report different amounts of cooling from tree cover. Before concluding that the findings conflict, a student adds “temperature measured” and “climate zone” columns to their matrix. Two studies measured air temperature and one measured surface temperature. The “conflict” turns into a finding about measurement, and the student notes that few studies compare both in the same city.

In an education course, a student synthesizes research on peer tutoring. Their matrix shows that positive effects cluster in studies with structured tutor training, while untrained-tutor studies show mixed results. They organize their review around tutor preparation rather than summarizing each study in turn.

Common pitfalls

  • One paragraph per source. If each paragraph begins with an author’s name, you’re probably summarizing.
  • Treating a few studies as consensus. Three studies don’t make “the literature.” Say how many sources support a claim and what kinds.
  • Hiding disagreement. Conflicting findings are often the most interesting part of a review. Explain them rather than leaving them out.
  • Losing your voice. The connections between studies are your contribution. Make them explicit.
  • Ignoring quality. Not all evidence weighs the same. Note design strength and sample size.

Keep in mind

Do not treat a small number of studies as field-wide consensus. Distinguish evidence (what studies found), interpretation (what you or the authors think it means), and uncertainty (what can’t yet be concluded). Scale your language to the evidence: suggests or indicates for limited evidence, and stronger verbs only when many well-designed studies agree.
