Peer Review and Revision

Give useful feedback and make intentional revision decisions.

Give useful feedback and make intentional revision decisions.

← All foundational skills

The essential idea: Peer review is collaborative inquiry into whether a piece of writing works for its purpose and audience. Revision rethinks meaning and structure. Copyediting improves expression once the larger decisions are sound.

Why peer review is worth your time

Students often assume the point of peer review is to get feedback. Research suggests that giving feedback may teach you as much or more. In one semester-long study, students who only reviewed others’ papers made larger gains in their own writing than students who only received feedback (Lundstrom & Baker, 2009). Interviews with university students point to a likely reason: writing a review makes you compare someone else’s work with your own and with the criteria, which sharpens your judgment of your own writing (Nicol, Thomson, & Breslin, 2014).

Peer review also helps with a common revision problem. Inexperienced writers tend to treat revision as fixing words and sentences, while experienced writers use it to reshape the argument (Sommers, 1980). A reader who asks “what is this paragraph for?” pushes the writer toward the second kind of revision.

Three levels of change

Name which level you’re working at, both as a reviewer and as a writer.

Level Focus Questions When
Revision (global) Purpose, argument, evidence, organization, audience Is the main point clear? Is it supported? Is the order logical? Is anything missing? Early drafts
Editing (paragraph and sentence) Clarity, flow, transitions, concision Does each paragraph have one job? Are sentences clear? Once the structure is stable
Proofreading (surface) Grammar, spelling, formatting, citations Are there errors? Does it follow the required style? Final draft

Work from the top down. Comma fixes in a paragraph that will be cut are wasted effort.

Core practices: giving feedback

1. Begin with the writer’s goal

Before reading, ask the writer (or have them write a short note):

  • What do you want readers to understand or do after reading?
  • Who is the intended audience?
  • What stage is this draft at?
  • What kind of feedback would be most useful right now? Where are you stuck or unsure?

This turns peer review from general judgment into a response to the writer’s own goals.

2. Read first, then respond

  • First pass: read without marking. Experience the draft as a reader would.
  • Summarize the main message in your own words. If you can’t, or if your summary differs from what the writer intended, that is your most important piece of feedback.
  • Second pass: annotate, tying each comment to a specific place in the draft.

A reverse outline of the draft (one line per paragraph describing what it does) is a useful thing to give a writer. See Outlining and Reverse Outlining.

3. Prioritize substantive issues

Comment first on focus, evidence, organization, audience, and argument. Leave sentence-level details for later drafts, or note patterns (“several sentences in section 2 begin with ‘This shows’”) rather than correcting every instance.

4. Make comments specific, located, and actionable

Vague Specific and useful
“Reorganize.” “Paragraphs 2 and 5 both cover land-use history. Combining them before your comparison in ¶4 might help readers follow the argument.”
“Good job!” “The example of the 2019 storm in ¶3 makes the stakes concrete. More examples like this in section 2 would help.”
“Unclear.” “I lost the thread in ¶6. Is the point that both methods fail, or that one works only in sheltered sites?”
“Needs more evidence.” “The claim in ¶4 that restoration costs are ‘much lower’ would be stronger with a figure or source.”

A useful pattern is observe → effect → suggest: “In ¶4 you give the recommendation before the data (observe). As a reader, I wasn’t yet convinced (effect). You could move the data in ¶6 ahead of it (suggest).”

5. Use reader-response language

Describe your experience as a reader (“I expected…”, “I got lost here…”, “I was persuaded by…”) rather than issuing verdicts (“this is wrong”). Reader-response comments are easier to hear and give the writer information they can act on.

6. Balance strength, question, and suggestion

For each review, aim for at least:

  • One strength to keep, named specifically so the writer knows what is working.
  • One significant question about the argument, evidence, or audience.
  • One specific revision suggestion, tied to a location in the draft.

Core practices: using feedback

1. Sort before you act

Read all the feedback once without responding. Then sort comments into:

  • Global (argument, structure, evidence) vs. local (sentences, mechanics)
  • Agree / Unsure / Disagree
  • Patterns: If two readers flag the same section, take it seriously, even if they suggest different fixes.

2. Write a revision plan

Turn comments into a short, ordered plan before changing the draft.

Priority Issue (from feedback) Decision Planned change Why
1 Recommendation appears before evidence (2 reviewers) Accept Move data from ¶6 ahead of ¶4 Readers need evidence first
2 Cost claim unsupported Adapt Add a source, and soften “much lower” to “lower” Only one cost study found
3 Reviewer suggests cutting the field-site description Decline Keep, but shorten Audience is unfamiliar with the site

3. Document your choices

Record why you accepted, adapted, or declined key suggestions. This helps you revise deliberately, and it is useful when a course asks for a revision memo or reflection.

4. Revise globally first, then locally

Address structure and argument first, then reread the whole draft before editing sentences.

A simple workflow

As a reviewer:

  1. Read the writer’s goals and questions.
  2. Read once without marking.
  3. Summarize the main message in two or three sentences.
  4. On a second pass, note one strength, one significant question, and one specific suggestion, each tied to a location.
  5. Add a reverse outline if structure seems to be the main issue.

As a writer:

  1. Tell reviewers your goal, your audience, and what you want feedback on.
  2. Read all feedback before responding.
  3. Sort comments and look for patterns.
  4. Write a revision plan with reasons.
  5. Revise globally, then edit and proofread.

In context

A reviewer notices that a restoration proposal gives its recommendation before showing the evidence. Instead of writing “reorganize,” they identify the relevant paragraphs, explain that they weren’t convinced when they first read the recommendation, and suggest putting the evidence first.

A student in a psychology course receives conflicting feedback. One peer wants more detail on methods, and another says the methods section is too long. Rather than choosing one, the writer notices that both reviewers struggled with the same section. They reorganize it with subheadings and move procedural details into a table. Their revision memo explains the decision.

Giving feedback with care

  • Critique the writing, not the writer. “This paragraph…” rather than “You…”
  • Respect the writer’s voice and experience, especially in personal or reflective writing. Don’t push a writer toward your own style.
  • Be honest and kind. Vague praise doesn’t help a writer improve, and harshness makes it harder to hear useful points.
  • Be aware of language and dialect bias. Don’t treat unfamiliar phrasing or a non-dominant variety of English as an error unless it actually gets in the way of meaning for the intended audience.

Keep in mind

Feedback is information, not an instruction to obey every suggestion. The writer remains responsible for the final piece. Good revision decisions consider each suggestion against the writer’s purpose, audience, and evidence.
