Communicating with Non-Expert Audiences

Make complex information useful without erasing nuance.

Make complex information useful without erasing nuance.

← All foundational skills

The essential idea: Good communication begins with a specific audience and purpose. Translating research for non-experts changes the language, structure, and format, while protecting the meaning and the limits of the evidence.

Beyond “just explain it more simply”

A common assumption is that people disagree with or ignore research because they lack information, so the fix is to give them more facts. This is called the knowledge deficit model. Research shows that public communication of science is more complicated than the model suggests, yet the model persists among scientists (Simis et al., 2016). People interpret information through their values, experiences, identities, and the decisions they face.

Effective communication therefore starts with the audience, not with your results. It also treats communication as a two-way exchange: communities, practitioners, and other non-specialists often hold knowledge that researchers lack.

Core practices

1. Know your audience and purpose

Before writing, answer:

  • Who specifically? (“The public” is too broad. Try “parents at a school board meeting,” “shellfish growers,” “city council staff,” or “readers of a campus newspaper.”)
  • What do they already know, and what do they care about?
  • What decisions might they face that your information affects?
  • What do you want them to know, feel, or do afterward?
  • What concerns or prior experiences might shape how they hear you?

When possible, ask: talk to someone from the audience, read what they read, or test a draft with them.

2. Lead with meaning

Scientific papers usually build from background through methods and results to the conclusion. Non-expert audiences generally want the reverse: the bottom line first, then why it matters, then as much supporting detail as they need.

Research paper order Non-expert order
Background → Methods → Results → Conclusion Main point → Why it matters → Key evidence → Details if needed

The Message Box. COMPASS’s Message Box is a widely used planning tool that organizes a message around an Issue and four questions: Problem, So what?, Solutions, and Benefits. It is adapted from Nancy Baron’s Escape from the Ivory Tower (COMPASS, n.d.). The “so what?” asks why this audience should care, and the answer often changes from one audience to another.

3. Choose words carefully

  • Replace or define jargon. Scientists often forget that they once didn’t know a term. This is sometimes called the “curse of knowledge.” Even lay summaries published alongside research articles contain more jargon than is recommended for public understanding (Rakedzon et al., 2017). Free tools such as the De-Jargonizer can flag uncommon words in your draft.
  • Watch for words that mean something different to the public. For example, “positive feedback” sounds like praise, “theory” sounds like a guess, “significant” sounds like “important,” and “uncertainty” sounds like “we don’t know.” Somerville and Hassol (2011) compiled a widely used table of such terms with better alternatives, for instance “human-caused” instead of “anthropogenic.”
  • Use concrete examples, comparisons, and familiar units. “About the weight of a paperclip” is easier to picture than “1 g.”
  • Define a necessary term once, clearly, and then use it consistently.

4. Use stories, with care

Narratives can increase comprehension, interest, and engagement, and most non-experts already get their science through media that favor stories (Dahlstrom, 2014). A specific person, place, or event can make a finding relevant. But stories are also persuasive, which raises ethical questions (Dahlstrom, 2014). Choose examples that represent the evidence fairly, not just the most dramatic case.

5. Communicate uncertainty honestly

Uncertainty is part of all knowledge, but communicators often avoid it for fear of losing trust. A review of the evidence found that communicating uncertainty does not necessarily affect audiences negatively, although effects vary with the person and the format (van der Bles et al., 2019). Practical guidance:

  • Quantify when you can: “between 10 and 15 percent” is clearer than “around 12 percent, though this is uncertain.”
  • Say what is known with confidence before what is uncertain.
  • Explain why something is uncertain (limited data, natural variability, models that differ) and what would reduce the uncertainty.
  • Keep the conditions: If a finding applies only to certain places, populations, or time periods, say so.

6. Match the medium

Choose a format because it fits the audience and the message, not out of habit.

Format Strengths Watch out for
Policy brief / fact sheet Fast, decision-focused Space forces hard choices about nuance
Infographic Visual, shareable Can oversimplify, and may not be accessible
Short talk or presentation Personal, allows questions One chance to land the main point
Blog post or article Room for story and context Needs a strong opening to hold readers
Social media post Reach Easy to lose conditions and caveats
Community meeting / workshop Two-way exchange Requires listening as much as presenting

7. Make it accessible and inclusive

  • Accessibility: readable fonts, sufficient color contrast, alt text for images, captions for video, and not relying on color alone. Plain language is an accessibility practice too.
  • Cultural context and values: Consider how your framing will land with different communities, and whose examples you choose.
  • Whose expertise counts: Acknowledge local, practical, and Indigenous knowledge. Credit community partners, and follow their guidance on what is shared and how (see Ethical Research, Writing, and AI Use).
  • Language access: Consider translation, and work with fluent or native speakers rather than relying only on automated translation for important messages.

A simple workflow

  1. Write a one-sentence takeaway: the single thing you most want the audience to remember.
  2. Name the specific audience and what they care about or need to decide.
  3. Fill in a Message Box (Problem, So what?, Solutions, Benefits) for that audience.
  4. Draft in “bottom line first” order.
  5. Replace or define specialized terms. Run a jargon check.
  6. Check fidelity: Compare your message with the original finding. Did you keep the conditions, limits, and uncertainty? Would the original researchers agree with your summary?
  7. Test it with someone from (or close to) the intended audience, and revise.

In context

For a community shoreline meeting, “hypoxia” becomes “low oxygen in the water,” followed by a concrete explanation of what that condition can mean for fish and shellfish. The speaker keeps an important limit: low-oxygen events were measured in summer at two sites, so the presentation doesn’t claim they happen everywhere or all year.

A public health student turns a study on heat and emergency visits into a one-page flyer for older adults. They lead with “Hot days increase the risk of heat illness, even indoors,” add three practical steps, and state the evidence in plain terms. They test the flyer with two residents at a senior center and enlarge the font after feedback.

Common pitfalls

  • Starting with methods or background the audience doesn’t need yet.
  • “Dumbing down” instead of clarifying, which can come across as condescending.
  • Dropping the caveats that change what a finding means.
  • Assuming a deficit: treating the audience as empty containers for facts rather than as people with knowledge, values, and concerns.
  • One message for every audience: The “so what?” usually needs to change.

Keep in mind

Avoid deficit assumptions about community knowledge. Consider accessibility, cultural context, values, and whose expertise is represented. Clear communication isn’t less rigorous. Getting it right requires understanding both the evidence and the audience.
